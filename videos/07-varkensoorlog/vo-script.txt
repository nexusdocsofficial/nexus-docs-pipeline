In the summer of 1859, the United States and the British Empire sent warships, cannons and hundreds of soldiers to one small island.

All because of a pig.

This is San Juan Island. Today it sits right on the border between the United States and Canada. And for twelve years, two rival armies lived on it at the same time.

Nobody won a battle. Nobody fired a single shot at the enemy. In the end, the whole thing was settled by the Emperor of Germany.

And on this island, American park rangers still raise the British flag. Every single day.

At the worst moment, British warships had their cannons aimed at the Americans on shore. And one British admiral refused to give the order. He would not start a war, he said, over a squabble about a pig.

So how did one pig bring two great powers to the edge of war? It all comes down to one question that nobody could answer: whose island is this?

Chapter one. One Channel Too Many.

And that question only existed because of a mistake on a map.

For years, America and Britain both claimed the same huge stretch of land in the Pacific Northwest. In 1846, they finally split it with a straight line: the 49th parallel, all the way to the sea.

But at the coast, there was a big island in the way: Vancouver Island. So the treaty said the line would turn, and run "through the middle of the channel" between the island and the mainland.

There was just one problem. There wasn't one channel. There were two.

Imagine two neighbours who agree that the fence will run down the middle of the garden. Then they walk outside, and discover that the garden has two paths.

The British said the border followed the eastern channel, Rosario Strait. The Americans said it followed the western one, Haro Strait. And right between those two channels lay a cluster of green islands: the San Juan Islands.

At first, nobody really cared. The Hudson's Bay Company, a giant British fur-trading company, moved in and started a sheep farm.

Then American settlers arrived, and built small farms on land the British company considered its own. By the spring of 1859, around eighteen Americans lived right next to the British farm.

Two nations. One island. And no fence. As long as nobody asked whose island it was, everything was fine.

But one of those Americans was about to have a very bad morning.

Chapter two. The Pig in the Potatoes.

Every war needs a first shot. This one had exactly one. And it hit a pig.

The man who fired it was Lyman Cutlar, an American farmer with a small potato patch. On the morning of June 15th, 1859, he found a large black pig in his garden, digging up his potatoes. And it wasn't the first time.

The pig belonged to the Hudson's Bay Company. Cutlar grabbed his rifle, and shot it.

Then he tried to do the decent thing. He went to the British farm manager, Charles Griffin, and offered to pay for the pig: ten dollars.

Griffin wanted a hundred.

Legend says Cutlar complained that the pig had been eating his potatoes. And Griffin answered that it was up to him to keep his potatoes out of the pig.

Cutlar refused to pay. And then British officials threatened to arrest him, and take him away to be tried in British territory.

And suddenly, it wasn't about a pig anymore. If Britain could arrest a man on this island, then it was a British island.

The garden with two paths suddenly needed an answer. Whose island is this?

The American settlers asked their army for protection. The message reached General William Harney, the American commander in the region, a man who strongly disliked the British. He saw his chance.

He ordered a young captain, George Pickett, to sail to the island with sixty-four soldiers.

Sixty-four soldiers on a quiet island full of sheep. Britain was not amused.

Chapter three. Warships Over a Pig.

This is the moment a dead pig almost started a real war.

Just across the water, in the city of Victoria, the British governor James Douglas was furious. He sent warships to San Juan Island, with orders to get the Americans off, but to avoid a fight if possible.

The Americans answered with more soldiers. Then the British brought more ships.

Within weeks, five British warships sat in the bay. On board were more than two thousand men, and dozens of cannons, pointed at the shore. On a hill above the beach, four hundred and sixty-one American soldiers dug in with fourteen cannons.

One wrong order. One nervous soldier. And the United States would be at war with the biggest empire on Earth.

And this is where the admiral from the beginning comes in. The commander of all British ships in the Pacific arrived: Rear Admiral Robert Baynes. He looked at the situation and refused to land his troops. He would not, he said, involve two great nations in a war over a squabble about a pig.

And while the generals argued, something strange happened on the island. The officers of both sides started getting along. They went to church together on a British ship. They shared whisky and cigars at the British farm.

In Washington, President James Buchanan was horrified. He sent his most senior general, Winfield Scott, on a six-week journey to calm things down. The two sides made a deal: most soldiers would go home, and each side would keep a small force on the island, until the diplomats found a solution.

The war was off. But the question was still open: whose island is this? And nobody expected the answer to take twelve years.

Chapter four. Twelve Years of Parties.

The strangest part of the Pig War isn't how it started. It's how long it lasted, and how friendly it became.

The Americans set up their camp at the windy southern end of the island: American Camp. The British Royal Marines built theirs in a sheltered bay in the north, with a garden and neat white buildings: English Camp.

Officially, they were enemies. In practice, they were neighbours.

They visited each other's holidays. The British came to celebrate the American Fourth of July. The Americans came over for Queen Victoria's birthday. There were dinners, sports and horse races.

Meanwhile, the rest of America was falling apart. When the Civil War broke out, Captain Pickett left the island to fight for the South, and later led one of the bloodiest attacks of the war: Pickett's Charge at Gettysburg. But on San Juan Island, the standoff stayed peaceful.

And that's where the German emperor comes in. In 1871, Britain and America agreed to let someone neutral answer the question for them: the Emperor of Germany, Wilhelm the First. His experts studied the maps and the old treaty for almost a year.

In October 1872, they gave their answer: the border follows Haro Strait. The San Juan Islands are American.

A month later, the British Royal Marines packed up and sailed away.

The final score of the Pig War: one pig. Zero people. And Lyman Cutlar never paid for it.

The question was answered. But the line from 1846 left behind more strange corners. And some of them are causing trouble right now.

Chapter five. The Border Isn't Finished.

Look just north of the islands. That same straight line along the 49th parallel cut off the tip of a small peninsula: Point Roberts.

It's American, but it's attached to Canada. To drive there from the rest of Washington State, you have to cross two international borders. About three quarters of the homes are owned by Canadians. Even the water comes from Canada.

So when a trade fight broke out between the two countries in 2025, and many Canadians stopped travelling south, this little American town felt it right away.

And the border itself still isn't finished. There are still a few places the two countries have never agreed on.

The most famous is a tiny rock off the coast of Maine: Machias Seal Island. It's home to thousands of seabirds, and a Canadian lighthouse that has stood there for about two hundred years. But in 2025, the American government said it clearly: Machias Seal Island is U.S. territory. Canada says it's Canadian.

It's the same question as on San Juan Island, more than a hundred and fifty years later: whose island is this?

And in September 2026, President Trump posted a map that showed all of North America as part of the United States.

The garden still has more than one path.

But the Pig War also shows another way. And Canada proved it again recently.

For decades, Canada and Denmark both claimed a bare rock near Greenland: Hans Island. Instead of fighting, their soldiers took turns visiting it, planting their flag and leaving a bottle for the other side. Canadian whisky. Danish schnapps. In 2022, they ended this so-called Whisky War by splitting the island in two.

So next time someone says two countries would never go to war over something small, remember San Juan Island. They almost did. Over a pig.

And remember that British flag from the beginning? Here's why it's still there.

Every morning at English Camp, an American park ranger walks to the flagpole, and raises the flag of the country that lost the island. Not to celebrate a victory. But to remember the war that never happened.

Because what stopped it wasn't a bigger army. It was one admiral who refused to give the order.

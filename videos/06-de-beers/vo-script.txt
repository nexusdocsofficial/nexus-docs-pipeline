In 2011, the most famous diamond company in the world was valued at almost thirteen billion dollars.

This year, its owner is trying to sell most of it. For about one billion.

For over a hundred years, this one company decided how many diamonds the world was allowed to buy. It helped turn a diamond ring into the rule for every proposal. It even gave you the sentence you probably know by heart.

But diamonds are not nearly as rare as their price makes you think. And now, the world is finding out.

So how did one company keep a whole planet believing, and what happens when the spell breaks?

Chapter one. A Shiny Pebble.

In 1867, a fifteen-year-old boy named Erasmus Jacobs picked up a shiny pebble near the Orange River in South Africa. It turned out to be a diamond of over twenty-one carats.

Within a few years, tens of thousands of diggers had rushed in. They dug so deep in one spot that it became a giant crater, and a town grew up around it: Kimberley.

And that was the problem. There were suddenly so many diamonds that prices started to fall.

Two men saw the danger. Cecil Rhodes, a young Englishman who had arrived with almost nothing, and Barney Barnato, a former music-hall performer from London. Both bought up mine after mine, and both kept digging, flooding the market.

In the end, Rhodes won. In 1888 he formed De Beers Consolidated Mines. And in 1889, he bought out Barnato's company with a cheque for more than five million pounds, one of the largest cheques ever written at the time.

Now one company owned almost every diamond mine in South Africa. And it had learned the most important lesson in the business:

The danger isn't too few diamonds. It's too many.

Chapter two. The Warehouse.

Imagine a company that prints trading cards. It prints millions. But it locks most of them in a warehouse, and lets out only a handful each month. Every kid on the playground thinks the cards are rare.

That's what De Beers did with diamonds.

In the 1890s, a group of London dealers agreed to buy diamonds only through De Beers, and to sell them slowly. Later, De Beers built its own selling office in London. Whenever someone discovered a new mine, anywhere in the world, De Beers tried to buy its diamonds too. Whatever it couldn't sell, it simply stored.

Too many diamonds coming in? Into the warehouse. Prices falling? Sell less.

Dealers were invited to London a few times a year. Each got a box of diamonds, and a price. You didn't haggle. If you said no, you might not be invited back.

By the late 1980s, this one office still controlled nearly eighty percent of the world's diamond trade.

But controlling supply was only half the trick. Someone still had to want the diamonds.

Chapter three. Forever.

In 1938, diamonds were selling badly in America. The country was just coming out of the Great Depression, and to many couples, a diamond ring was a luxury they could skip.

So Harry Oppenheimer, the son of De Beers' chairman, went to New York and hired an advertising agency, N. W. Ayer.

Their plan wasn't to sell one brand of diamond. It was to sell an idea: that real love comes with a diamond. They put diamonds on movie stars and in magazines. They sent speakers to schools to talk about engagement rings.

Then, in 1947, a young copywriter named Frances Gerety wrote four words: "A Diamond Is Forever."

Half a century later, Advertising Age named it the best slogan of the twentieth century.

And "forever" did a clever second job. If a diamond is forever, you never sell it. And if nobody sells their old diamonds, they never come back on the market to compete with new ones.

Later ads even told men how much to spend: about one month's salary.

It worked far beyond America. In Japan in 1967, fewer than one in twenty brides got a diamond ring. By 1981, it was about six in ten.

The warehouse kept the cards scarce. The ads made everyone want a card.

But a warehouse only works if you can buy up every new card that's printed.

Chapter four. The Cracks.

In the 1980s and 1990s, big new mines opened that De Beers didn't control. In Australia, the Argyle mine. Then in Canada, in the far north. Russia's diamond company kept a deal with De Beers for years, but in 2009, it ended it.

Keeping prices high meant buying ever more diamonds and storing them. By the end of the 1990s, De Beers held a stockpile worth billions.

So around 2000, it changed course. It sold off much of the stockpile and stopped trying to control the whole market. Its share of world sales fell below sixty percent, and kept falling.

The warehouse doors were open.

And then came the real threat: a factory.

Scientists had learned to grow diamonds in a lab. Not fake stones. Real diamonds, made of the same carbon, with the same sparkle. Even experts need a machine to tell them apart.

At first, De Beers warned that lab diamonds weren't "real". Then, in 2018, it started selling its own, under the name Lightbox, at eight hundred dollars a carat. That was a fraction of the price of a mined stone.

In other words, De Beers itself told the world what a diamond costs when nobody holds supply back.

Lab diamond prices kept falling. Between 2020 and 2024, a one-carat lab diamond lost about three quarters of its price.

And couples noticed. In 2025, most American couples who got married chose a lab-grown diamond as the center stone. Sixty-one percent.

The trading cards weren't rare anymore. Anyone could print them.

Chapter five. Is Forever Over?

Now the old diamonds are falling too. A typical one-carat natural diamond lost about thirty percent of its price between 2021 and 2025. And De Beers' own rough diamond prices fell about a quarter in 2025 alone.

De Beers closed Lightbox in 2025. It says it now wants to focus only on natural diamonds. And it has paused production at one of its biggest mines in South Africa, for about two years.

Its owner, the mining giant Anglo American, took control in 2011, in a deal that valued De Beers at almost thirteen billion dollars. In 2024, Anglo put it up for sale. By the end of 2025, Anglo valued it at just two point three billion. And in July 2026, reports said Anglo was in advanced talks to sell its majority stake for about one billion, to a group led by a former De Beers boss.

One country is watching closely: Botswana. Most of De Beers' diamonds now come from its mines, and diamonds pay a big part of the country's bills. Botswana already owns part of De Beers, and it has the right to step in.

So what does this mean for the ring on your finger?

A diamond can still be beautiful, and it can still mean a lot. But try selling one, and many buyers will offer you only a part of what you paid.

For more than a century, one company made the world believe that diamonds are rare, and that a diamond is forever.

The company may not last forever. But the idea might.
